# Searing Techniques

The sear creates the crust and Maillard reaction that makes food visually appealing and loaded with flavor. This finishing step is essential — it transforms perfectly cooked protein into something memorable.

---

## The Purpose of Searing

After sous vide, your food is perfectly cooked to target temperature throughout. The sear adds:

- **Color** — the golden-brown crust we associate with cooked meat
- **Flavor** — Maillard reaction creates hundreds of new flavor compounds
- **Texture contrast** — the crisp crust against the tender interior
- **Aroma** — the smell triggers appetite

You cannot skip this step if you want restaurant-quality results.

---

## Understanding Maillard Reaction

The Maillard reaction occurs when amino acids meet reducing sugars at temperatures above 280°F (138°C). This is:

- **Not carmelization** — that's sugar-specific
- **Not burning** — that's pyrolysis (bitter)
- **A complex chemical reaction** — creates the "cooked" flavor we crave

The sear must be hot enough to trigger this reaction quickly, before the interior overcooks.

---

## Torch Method

The culinary torch gives precise control. No pan cleanup, no smoke.

### Best For

- Delicate proteins: chicken breast, fish, thin cuts
- Finishing pan-seared steaks (the top side)
- Odd shapes that don't fit pans
- Presentation before guests

### Technique

1. **Dry your protein thoroughly** — moisture is the enemy of searing. Pat with paper towels and let rest 15–30 minutes.

2. **Stabilize the flame** — Light the torch and wait 30 seconds until the flame is steady and blue.

3. **Hold the proper distance** — 2–4 inches from the surface. Too close burns; too far wastes heat.

4. **Keep moving** — Continuous motion prevents hot spots. Sweep across the entire surface.

5. **Rotate for thick cuts** — Brown each side individually. The torch won't render fat like a pan can.

### Why It Works

The torch raises internal temperature minimally (5–10°F). This lets you add color without cooking the interior past your target. It gives you control that pan-searing doesn't.

## Cast Iron Method

The traditional approach. Produces the best crust and lets you work with fat rendering.

### Best For

- Thick cuts: ribeye, porterhouse, tomahawk
- Rendering fat: the heat renders marbling into the meat
- Butter basting: flavor infusion
- Indoor cooking with ventilation

### Technique

1. **Preheated is essential** — Heat your cast iron in a 450°F (232°C) oven for 20–30 minutes before use.

2. **Get the pan screaming hot** — Test with a drop of water — it should sizzle instantly and evaporate.

3. **Dry your protein** — As with torch method.

4. **Oil the pan, not the protein** — A thin layer of high smoke-point oil (avocado, grapeseed) coats the pan.

5. **Don't touch** — Let the protein sit undisturbed for 30–45 seconds. This builds the crust.

6. **Flip once** — One flip, one rest per side.

7. **Baste (optional)** — Add butter, garlic, herbs after flipping for flavor infusion.

8. **Rest before slicing** — 2–5 minutes lets juices redistribute.

### Why It Works

Cast iron retains heat exceptionally well. The initial sear continues cooking through the flip. Fat renders and bastes the surface naturally.

## Common Mistakes

### Steaming Instead of Searing

The pan wasn't hot enough, or the protein wasn't dry enough. The result: gray, soggy exterior instead of crust.

**Fix:** Ensure pan is truly hot (test with water). Dry protein thoroughly with paper towels. Pat dry again if needed.

### Overcrowding

Too much food in the pan drops the temperature dramatically. Steam cooks rather than sears.

**Fix:** Cook one protein at a time. Allow space between pieces.

### Moving Too Soon

Flipping repeatedly prevents crust formation. The protein steams in its own moisture.

**Fix:** Let it sit undisturbed. 30–45 seconds minimum before the first flip.

### Wrong Oil

Low smoke-point oils burn and create bitter flavors.

**Fix:** Use avocado oil (520°F smoke point), grapeseed oil, or refined canola.

## Pro Tips

- **Dry is everything** — Pat dry, rest uncovered in fridge 30 minutes before searing if possible
- **Hot first, then sear** — Preheating is non-negotiable
- **Rest after searing** — 2–5 minutes lets juices redistribute
- **Combine methods** — Sear one side in pan, torch the top for best of both worlds
