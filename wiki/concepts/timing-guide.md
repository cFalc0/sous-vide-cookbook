# Timing Guide

Unlike conventional cooking where proteins go from raw to perfectly cooked to overcooked in minutes, sous vide allows precise control over cooking duration—but this flexibility comes with its own rules. The timing window for each protein depends on whether you are cooking for doneness (reaching target temperature), pasteurization (food safety), or tenderization (collagen conversion). Understanding these three phases and their interaction with temperature is the key to consistent sous vide results.

## Summary

Sous vide cooking times range from 30 minutes for delicate fish to 72 hours for tough cuts like beef short ribs. Timing depends on two factors: the protein type (which determines its collagen content and texture goals) and the thickness of the cut (which affects how long the center takes to reach the target temperature). The core principle is that sous vide cooks from edge to edge uniformly, so time is governed by thickness, not weight. For food safety, cooks below 130°F (54°C) should not exceed 4 hours total, while tougher cuts benefit from 24-72 hour cooks at higher temperatures to convert collagen into tender gelatin.

## Details

### The Three Goals of Sous Vide Timing

Understanding why you are cooking something sous vide determines how long it should stay in the bath. There are three distinct timing goals:

1. **Cooking for Doneness**: Getting the center to the target temperature. This is the minimum time needed for heat to penetrate from the surface to the center of the food. This typically takes 30 minutes to 3 hours depending on thickness.

2. **Pasteurization**: Eliminating harmful bacteria to make the food safe. This requires specific time-temperature combinations—longer times at lower temperatures or shorter times at higher temperatures achieve equivalent safety. This is measured in log reductions (D-values), where a 6D reduction means 1,000,000:1 reduction in pathogens.

3. **Tenderization**: Breaking down tough connective tissue (collagen) into silky gelatin. This is the sous vide superpower—it allows tough cuts to become fork-tender while maintaining a pink, medium-rare center. Collagen conversion requires 6-72 hours depending on temperature and cut.

Every sous vide cook addresses all three goals to some degree, but the emphasis changes based on what you are cooking.

### The 4-Hour Rule and Food Safety

The USDA Food Code states that food must not remain in the "temperature danger zone" (40°F to 130°F / 4°C to 54°C) for more than 4 hours total, including both the heating and cooling phases. This is the foundation of the 4-hour rule:

- **Cooks below 130°F (54°C)**: Maximum 4 hours in the water bath. This applies to rare steak (120-129°F), very rare fish (105-115°F), and any cook targeting rare doneness. If you need longer for tenderization, raise the temperature to 130°F or higher.

- **Cooks at 130°F (54°C) and above**: Can safely hold food for extended periods (24-72 hours). These temperatures are high enough to inhibit pathogen growth during longer cooks, making them safe for tough cuts that need prolonged tenderization.

This rule is why beef tenderloin cooked at 120°F for rare is limited to 2-4 hours, while beef short ribs cooked at 130°F or higher can go for 48-72 hours without safety concerns.

### The 24-Hour Rule: Texture Transformation

Beyond food safety lies a second threshold: the 24-hour mark. This is where texture changes become significant:

- **Before 24 hours**: Muscle fibers remain intact. The meat becomes progressively more tender as collagen softens and natural enzymes (calpains and cathepsins) break down some connective tissue. Texture shifts from firm to buttery-soft.

- **After 24 hours**: Muscle fibers themselves begin to break down. The texture becomes notably softer, approaching a "mushy" quality for tender cuts. Tough cuts continue improving as collagen fully converts to gelatin.

This is why a ribeye steak cooked for 4 hours at 131°F is "buttery-soft" but one cooked for 24 hours might be unpleasantly soft. Filet mignon and other already-tender cuts do not benefit from—and can actually suffer from—cooks beyond 4-6 hours.

For tough cuts like short ribs, chuck roast, and pork shoulder, 24-72 hours is the sweet spot where collagen fully converts to gelatin and the meat becomes impossibly tender while retaining its pink color.

### How Time Affects Texture: The Science

The texture changes during sous vide are driven by what happens to proteins at different temperatures over time:

**Myosin Denaturation (122-131°F / 50-55°C)**: The first protein to denature is myosin, which makes up about 55% of muscle protein. When it denatures, meat firms slightly but remains tender. This is the "medium-rare" transition zone—desirable for steak.

**Collagen Shrinkage and Conversion (131-154°F / 55-68°C)**: Collagen proteins begin shrinking around 131°F but only convert to gelatin above 154°F (68°C). However, at lower temperatures (130-140°F / 54-60°C), enzymes remain active for hours and slowly break down collagen. This is why tough cuts become tender over 24-72 hours without the meat cooking beyond medium-rare.

- At 130°F / 54°C: Full collagen conversion takes 48-72 hours
- At 140°F / 60°C: Full collagen conversion takes 18-24 hours
- At 158°F / 70°C: Full collagen conversion takes 8-12 hours
- At 167°F / 75°C: Full collagen conversion takes 3-6 hours

Higher temperatures accelerate conversion but also cook the meat more, creating a trade-off.

**Actin Denaturation (151-158°F / 66-70°C)**: When actin denatures, muscle fibers contract sharply and squeeze out moisture. This is why well-done meat is dry. Holding meat above 158°F for extended periods progressively drives out moisture.

The key insight: You cannot have both a pink medium-rare interior and maximum tenderness in the same cut unless you use low temperatures (130-140°F / 54-60°C) combined with long cook times (24-72 hours). This is the magic of sous vide short ribs—filet-tender texture with steak-like color.

### Timing by Protein: Beef

Beef timing depends entirely on whether you are cooking a tender cut or a tough cut:

| Cut Type | Temperature | Time Range | Texture Goal |
|----------|-------------|------------|---------------|
| **Tender Cuts** (Ribeye, Strip, Filet) | | | |
| - Rare | 120-128°F (49-53°C) | 1-2.5 hours | Heat through |
| - Medium-Rare | 129-134°F (54-57°C) | 1-4 hours (2.5 hrs max below 130°F) | Heat through + pasteurize |
| - Medium | 135-144°F (57-62°C) | 1-4 hours | Heat through |
| - Medium-Well | 145-155°F (63-68°C) | 1-3.5 hours | Heat through |
| - Well Done | 156°F+ (69°C+) | 1-3 hours | Heat through |
| **Tough Cuts** (Brisket, Chuck, Short Ribs) | | | |
| - Medium-Rare Texture | 130°F (54°C) | 24-72 hours | Tenderize (pink center) |
| - Traditional Braise | 155-167°F (68-75°C) | 12-36 hours | Fork-tender |

For tender cuts, timing is based on thickness. Add 30-45 minutes per inch of thickness. A 1-inch steak needs 1-2 hours; a 2-inch steak needs 3-4 hours.

For tough cuts going for the "medium-rare texture" result (filet-tender but pink), cook at 130°F for 48-72 hours. For traditional fork-tender braised texture, cook at 155-167°F for 24-36 hours.

### Timing by Protein: Pork

Pork sits in the middle—it has moderate collagen and is typically cooked to medium (slightly pink) or well-done.

| Cut | Temperature | Time Range | Notes |
|-----|-------------|------------|-------|
| Pork Chops | 140-145°F (60-63°C) | 1-4 hours | 1-inch chops: 1-2 hrs; 2-inch: 3-4 hrs |
| Pork Tenderloin | 140-145°F (60-63°C) | 1-3 hours | Very lean; avoid overcooking |
| Pork Loin (roast) | 140-145°F (60-63°C) | 4-8 hours | Add 45 min/inch thickness |
| Pork Belly | 150°F (65°C) | 12-24 hours | Soft, silken texture |
| Pork Ribs (spareribs) | 150°F (65°C) | 12-24 hours | Fall-off-the-bone tender |
| Pork Shoulder/Butt | 150°F (65°C) | 12-24 hours | Pulled pork texture |
| Pork Chops (well done) | 158°F (70°C) | 2-4 hours | Traditional firm texture |

The "4-hour rule" applies to pork: if cooking below 130°F, limit to 4 hours. For standard medium (140-145°F), 1-4 hours is the safe window.

### Timing by Protein: Poultry

Poultry must be pasteurized for food safety. This requires both sufficient temperature and time.

| Cut | Temperature | Time Range | Notes |
|-----|-------------|------------|-------|
| Chicken Breast | 140°F (60°C) | 1-4 hours | Soft, juicy |
| Chicken Breast (firm) | 149°F (65°C) | 1-3 hours | More traditional texture |
| Chicken Thighs | 150°F (65°C) | 2-4 hours | Skin-soft, not rubbery |
| Chicken Thighs (fall-off-bone) | 165°F (74°C) | 2-4 hours | Traditional texture |
| Chicken Legs | 150°F (65°C) | 2-4 hours | |
| Whole Chicken | 140-145°F (60-63°C) | 4-6 hours | 4-5 lb bird |
| Duck Breast | 135°F (57°C) | 2-4 hours | Medium-rare; rich fat renders |
| Duck Legs | 155°F (68°C) | 8-18 hours | Tender, not stringy |
| Turkey Breast | 140°F (60°C) | 2-4 hours | Boneless, 1-2 inches thick |

**Critical**: Chicken should reach 140°F (60°C) and hold for pasteurization. According to Baldwin's research, at 140°F, a 1-inch thick chicken breast achieves a 6D reduction of Listeria in about 1 hour and 15 minutes. Thicker cuts need more time.

The texture window for chicken is narrower than beef: beyond 4 hours, chicken can become mushy due to enzyme activity breaking down the meat already tender at pasteurization.

### Timing by Protein: Fish and Seafood

Fish is delicate and lacks significant collagen. Time goals here are different—fish is about cooking to temperature, not tenderization.

| Fish Type | Temperature | Time Range | Texture |
|-----------|-------------|------------|---------|
| Fatty Fish (Salmon) | | | |
| - Translucent | 105°F (40°C) | 30-45 min | Sashimi-like |
| - Soft/flaky | 122°F (50°C) | 45-60 min | Buttery, opaque |
| - Firm/flaky | 135°F (57°C) | 45-60 min | Traditional cooked |
| Lean White Fish (Cod, Halibut) | 130-135°F (54-57°C) | 30-45 min | Flake, moist |
| Tuna | 105°F (40°C) | 30-45 min | Rare, sear-able |
| Shellfish | | | |
| - Scallops | 130°F (54°C) | 30 min | Plump, tender |
| - Shrimp | 145°F (63°C) | 30 min | Firm, not rubbery |
| - Lobster | 135°F (57°C) | 30-45 min | Tender, not tough |
| - Octopus | 170°F (76°C) | 4-8 hours | Ultra-tender |

Fish can be overcooked in sous vide. Unlike beef where extra time improves texture, fish becomes progressively worse with longer cooks. The proteins in fish are more delicate and the collagen converts to gelatin at much lower temperatures (around 104°F / 40°C) than land animals. This is why fish "falls apart" at temperatures that barely warm beef.

For fish, aim for minimum time to reach temperature plus a small buffer (15-30 minutes). Do not hold fish at low temperatures for more than 2 hours.

Vegetables do not have the same food safety concerns as proteins. The goal is simply even heat penetration to create uniform texture.

### Thickness Adjustment: The Golden Rule

All timing in sous vide scales with thickness. The general rule:

- **Add 30-45 minutes per inch of thickness** for steaks and chops
- **Add 30-60 minutes** if starting from frozen
- **Minimum time** = time for center to reach target temperature
- **Maximum time** = when texture begins degrading

| Thickness | Minimum Time (to temp) | Maximum Time (before texture change) |
|-----------|----------------------|--------------------------------|
| 0.5 inch (13mm) | 30 minutes | 2.5 hours |
| 1 inch (25mm) | 1 hour | 4 hours |
| 1.5 inches (38mm) | 1.75 hours | 5 hours |
| 2 inches (50mm) | 3 hours | 6 hours |
| 2.5 inches (63mm) | 4.25 hours | 7 hours |

These times are estimates; actual time depends on starting temperature (refrigerated vs. frozen), water circulation, and fat content. Use a guide and test with your specific setup.

### Quick Reference: The Practical Guide

**For a Quick Weeknight Meal** (30 min - 1 hour):
- Fish (lean white fish, salmon): 45 minutes at 130-135°F
- Shrimp: 30 minutes at 145°F
- Vegetables: 30-45 minutes at 183-185°F

**For Weekend Dinner** (1-4 hours):
- Steak (1-inch): 1-2 hours at 131°F (medium-rare)
- Chicken breast: 1-2 hours at 140°F
- Pork chops: 1-2 hours at 140°F
- Duck breast: 2-3 hours at 135°F

**For Extraordinary Tenderization** (12-72 hours):
- Short ribs: 48-72 hours at 130°F (medium-rare, fork-tender)
- Brisket: 48-72 hours at 130°F (medium-rare texture)
- Pork belly: 12-24 hours at 150°F
- Pork ribs: 12-24 hours at 150°F
- Pork shoulder: 12-24 hours at 150°F
- Chuck roast: 24-48 hours at 140°F

**For Traditional Braised Texture** (8-24 hours):
- Beef short ribs: 24 hours at 167°F
- Lamb shanks: 24 hours at 170°F
- Oxtail: 12-18 hours at 170°F
- Chicken thighs: 2-4 hours at 165°F
